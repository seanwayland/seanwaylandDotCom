Sound

Left / Right engines

With Split on, keys below the split point play the Left engine only and keys from it up play the Right one — two instruments under one keyboard. Off, both engines play everything, as they always have. Each slot's Transpose shifts what that side plays, up to two octaves either way; a note already sounding keeps the side and the pitch it started on. Saved with the patch.
Each slot has its own pulse width (only audible when that slot is set to Pulse) and Timbre offset (64 = no offset; the mod wheel still moves both slots together, this just biases one slightly brighter/darker than the other). Pan defaults hard-left/hard-right — bring either toward the middle to blend it across both ears.

Filter routing & tracking — how the two sides connect, saved per patch

Each side has its own filter, in its column above: pick Chrome 2-pole there and its cutoff, resonance, LP/HP, drive and FM sit with it. The two controls here are the ones that aren't per side.
Patch routing — how the two sides' filters are wired to each other. Independent: each side through its own. Series: both oscillators into A, then into B — four poles, and the two resonances stack. Parallel: both into both. Variable mix: mixed into both, each keeping its own level, which is what makes a notch when one side is low-pass and the other high-pass. Ring modulation multiplies the two sides instead of summing them, then runs the result through filter A. It is the sum and difference of the two, so it only makes a sound when BOTH sides are playing, and detuning them or playing different notes is where it gets interesting. Filter FM routes the B side's output into the A filter's cutoff.

Hard sync between the two sides is deliberately not offered: sync resets one oscillator's phase when another wraps, and these are two independent polyphonic engines whose voices are allocated separately, so there is no single phase on either side to sync to. The Chrome VCO does have sync between its own A and B oscillators.
Mod wheel — how far the wheel moves each of these, saved per patch. Centre is none; either side of centre sets how much, and which way, so the wheel can close a filter as readily as open it. A parameter sits exactly where its own control is set until a depth here says otherwise.

The wheel used to reach four of these on its own and at a fixed amount — Timbre on every engine, the filter cutoff by a full three octaves, a Chrome VCO's pulse width, and the Six-O-Bee/Two-Rev shape — with no way to say how much. That is gone, so a patch that has not been set up here ignores the wheel entirely. The two Waylinator engines are the exception and are deliberately untouched: the wheel still drives their cutoff and filter-envelope depth directly, which is a large part of how they sound.

Engine editors

4-Op PM operators — saved per patch

The reface-style operator page for the 4-Op PM engine — one for each side, so Left and Right can be two different FM sounds at once. Turn on Manual edit and that side's selected preset is copied there to start from; changing preset while it's on loads that preset instead. Off, the presets play as they are. Each operator's own envelope only applies while the slot's ADSR box is off. A side only shows while that slot is set to 4-Op PM.

Modelled Rhodes — per patch, per slot

Every control here moves a physical quantity, not an effect. Pickup distance and offset move the magnet relative to the tine, so they change the harmonics, the bark and the attack together — dead centre the fundamental cancels, which is a real pickup effect. Hammer sets the contact time, which is what makes a soft note round and a hard one bell-like. Bark is how far the tine swings, so it reaches the nonlinearity through displacement rather than by adding distortion. The mod matrix routes velocity or note number onto any of them, including places no screwdriver could reach — the pickup gap closing as you play harder is not physical, and that is the point.

Drawbar organ & rotary — saved per patch

The registration for the Drawbar Organ engine — one for each side, so Left and Right can be two different organs at once — and the rotary speaker that sits at the very end of the chain (it works on everything, not just the organ, and is shared). Percussion is single-trigger like the real thing: it only fires again once you've let go of every key. A side only shows while that slot is set to Drawbar Organ.
Touch off is the real instrument: a key is a switch. On, velocity sets each note's level, shaped by the Drawbar Organ's velocity curve below. Perc volume scales the percussion on top of Soft/Normal; 100 is where it always sat. Vibrato is the scanner: V1–V3 are progressively deeper vibrato, C1–C3 the same mixed with the dry organ for the chorus.
Rotary speaker — horn and drum turn at different speeds and take a moment to spin up or coast down. The mod wheel switches it live: down = Stop, middle = Slow, up = Fast. Whichever you touched last wins — the wheel, or the Speed button below. Wet at 0 is bypass, and speed changes are inaudible there.

Slow speed and Fast speed multiply whatever the chosen cabinet turns at, rather than setting an absolute rate — so each model keeps its own character and x1.00 is the cabinet untouched. Ramp scales how fast the motors get there; spin-up and coast-down move together, because a Leslie coasts down slower than it winds up and that lag is most of the effect. All three are saved per patch.

Envelope, filter & modulation

Global ADSR — saved per patch

Reaches Sine/Saw/Square/Pulse, CDG Rotator, Waylo Xfade2's square half, and the sample voices (Vintage Vibe, Waylo Tele, Sustain Steel and the SD instruments) — on those, Attack sets the fade-in length and Release the fade-out, with Decay/Sustain unused since the recording provides its own decay. Not mda/FM/Guitar, which have their own envelopes. Attack also sets how quickly a sample speaks: at the factory default (2) every sampled note fades in over ~31ms.

Velocity & effects — saved per patch (master volume: global)

Master tone — after everything, just before the output. This is a TONE control, not an anti-aliasing filter: it cannot undo aliasing, because a folded component is already sitting at an audible frequency by the time it gets here and nothing downstream can separate it from real signal. What it does fix is the ordinary thing that gets mistaken for aliasing — real harmonics passing a gentle filter. Measured on the Waylo Six-O-Bee with its cutoff at 1929 Hz playing middle C, the 10th to 18th harmonics sit between −38 and −53 dB a few hundred Hz apart, which is exactly what “fizzy” describes. Roll-off is an 8th-order Butterworth, 48 dB/octave, flat below the corner. 20 kHz is there so you can confirm for yourself that it does almost nothing. Top cut is a gentler alternative: a high shelf from about 4 kHz, which thins the fizz without putting a blanket over the instrument the way a brickwall at 5 kHz does.

The Pod's output level, after every effect — the one setting that stays global, the same in every patch. 0dB is where it always sat; up to +10.8dB, which can clip on loud patches.
How hard MIDI velocity drives level, across every engine. 1.00 is raw velocity (how it behaved before); higher pushes soft hits further down so playing dynamics count for more.
Key sliders, measured from middle C: + brighter going up, − brighter going down (and darker going up). Key → Filter moves filter cutoff on the synths and a brightness tilt on the sampled instruments; Key → FM moves FM modulation depth and feedback (DX10, 4-Op, FM Rhds, Xfade2) and pulse width. Vel → Tone moves both: + harder is brighter, − harder is darker. Vel → Filter is the filter side on its own: it moves filter cutoff on the synths and the brightness tilt on the sampled instruments, on top of Vel → Tone. Vel → FM is the same but for the FM engines only (DX10, 4-Op PM, FM Rhds, Xfade2 — and pulse width on Pulse), on top of Vel → Tone. Vel → Feedback moves operator feedback, which only the 4-Op PM engine has: + harder bites harder, and it's capped where feedback turns to noise. Vel boost multiplies velocity up to ×4: soft playing gets louder and brighter, and hard playing goes past full level, so it can clip on purpose.
Saved per patch. Classic is the old Waylinator synth's chorus and delay, exactly: knob 1 is the chorus's dry/wet, depth and rate together, knob 2 the three fixed delay taps (0.75s centre, left and right, their own feedback). The sliders below only set the WAYLORD ones.
Chorus — knob 1 sweeps up to the wet level.
Delay — knob 2 sweeps up to the wet and feedback levels.
Amp — a guitar amplifier: pickup EQ, a cocked wah, supply sag, an oversampled valve-ish clipping stage and a speaker cabinet. First in the chain, so the modulation and delay below sit after it, the way a real rig is wired. Works on anything — the sampled guitars, the FM engines, the organ, or the Pod's audio input. Mix at 0 is bypass, and costs nothing. The ↳ sliders say how far the mod wheel, velocity or note number moves the control above them: centre is none, right adds as the source rises, left subtracts. Velocity runs from nothing at a soft touch to full at 127; note number is centred on middle C, so the bottom of the keyboard pulls one way and the top the other. Velocity and note follow the last note played — the amp sits after the mix, so that is the only thing they can mean. The mod wheel sliders say how far the wheel moves the control above: centre is none, right pushes up as the wheel rises, left pulls down — so the wheel can clean a sound up as well as dirty it. Wah freq moves by up to two octaves either way.
one amp instead of two — saves about 6% CPU of the amp's 16%, measured on the device. A real guitar amp is mono; this does collapse a stereo source.
the instruments stop following the mod wheel and sit at the Timbre below — the wheel then drives the amp alone
Phaser — four allpass stages swept smoothly. Classic uses every slider below; Phase 90 models the MXR pedal (script logo), so only Wet and Rate (its Speed knob) apply. Wet at 0 is bypass.
Reverb — last in the chain. Plate: Dattorro's plate. Hall: an 8-line feedback delay network, long and smooth (Decay: about 0.8s to 8s). Room: Freeverb, shorter and denser. Wet at 0 is bypass.
Harmonic tremolo — fades between the bands either side of the crossover in antiphase, so the wobble moves through the spectrum rather than just the level.
Ring mod — mda RingMod: the mix is multiplied by a sine. Wet at 0 is bypass.

Audio input & output — input per patch, mono output for the instrument

Whatever is plugged into the Pod's inputs goes through the same effects as the engines. Level 0 is off; 100 is unity and above that it drives into clipping on purpose. Mono input takes the left input to both sides, for a guitar or anything else on one jack. Turn both slots' Vol down to use the Pod as an effects box. Mono output sends the same sum out of both jacks and the headphones — for a single channel on a desk. It is per patch now, like the In/out mode below, so one patch can be a mono pedal and the next a stereo synth.
This is saved with the patch, so one patch can be a mono effects pedal and the next a stereo synth. In the effects-only modes the patch's own effects still do the work — a patch with just phaser and reverb is a phaser and reverb pedal — the engines simply aren't heard. The Input level below still sets how much input; a patch that never set one gets unity.

Tapping the Pod's encoder bypasses the engines and lets the input through the patch's effects; tapping again brings them back. Two states, not six — stepping through all six live was a good way to end up somewhere you didn't intend, with only an LED colour to tell you. The LEDs go red while bypassed. Bypass is never saved: a patch always loads un-bypassed. Holding the encoder still jumps back to patch 1.

MIDI — the instrument, not the patch

These belong to the instrument rather than to a patch: a receive channel that changed when you changed patch would be a trap. Receive channel at Omni answers everything. Ignore CC stops an external controller reaching the patch — the NRPN transport still works, which is deliberate, because NRPN is how this switch is turned back off; and the mod wheel, sustain, expression and volume still work, because those are how the instrument is played rather than edited. Each slot's own channel is with that slot's controls.

Save, load & device

Device — flash-backed, survives power off

Save commits whatever's currently live and writes all 10 patch slots to the Pod's flash in one go. On Connect, this page reads the 10 patches back FROM the device.
Init patch sets the patch you have selected to a bare starting point — a sine on both sides, and every effect, filter, routing and modulation at neutral, so the mod wheel does nothing until you give it a depth. It is the thing to start a new sound from, and the thing to fall back to when a patch has drifted somewhere odd and you want to hear what one change actually does. It only changes what is live: nothing reaches flash until you press Save.
Banks on this computer — a bank is everything the Pod holds: all 10 patches plus every global setting (effects, organ, 4-Op, velocity curves, sample starts, tracking). Save downloads it as a file; Load sends a file's bank to the Pod, which takes around half a minute because each patch's instruments load from the SD card on the way. Loading replaces what's live but doesn't write flash — press Save all patches to device afterwards to keep it.
One patch on this computer — just the patch you have selected (both slots and everything saved with the patch, including its effects), not the globals. Save downloads it; Load sends it into whichever patch is selected at the time, so you can move a patch from one slot to another, or from one bank to another. Like a bank, loading replaces what's live — press Save all patches to device to keep it.
Update the firmware — needs Chrome or Edge. The Pod is asked to reboot into its bootloader, the file is written, and it restarts on its own. On Windows the bootloader needs a WinUSB driver (Zadig) before the browser can talk to it. If an update is interrupted the Pod sits in its bootloader and nothing is lost: run this again.

Per instrument — saved with Save all patches, applies in every patch

Start is how far into its recording a sampled instrument begins; defaults are the measured silence before each one actually speaks (Vintage Vibe had ~28ms), which is what made them feel late. Attack is the shape of the fade-in, whose length is the ADSR Attack knob: Linear rises in a straight line, Exponential starts slowly and arrives quickly, which softens the front of a note without lengthening it. It covers the sampled instruments, the FM engines and the synths with a real attack (Sine/Saw/Square/Pulse, Rotator, Xfade2, FM Rhds, Waylo Guitar) — on the DX10, Linear means mda's own curve. New instruments default to Exponential attack and S-curve velocity. Velocity shapes how hard playing translates to level for that instrument, on top of the global Velocity slider. Start only applies to sampled instruments; Sustain Steel's fade is a real ADSR, so it has no attack choice.

Keyboard map — per patch

Drag the dots to make a range of the keyboard harder or louder. Nine points, one an octave from C0 to C8, straight lines between them. Velocity adds to the incoming velocity, so on a sampled instrument it picks a harder recording as well as a louder one — that is usually what "louder" means on a piano. Level is plain gain in dB, and it is the only one that does anything on the organ or the analogue engines, where velocity is ignored unless Touch is on. Shift‑drag for fine control, double‑click a dot to flatten it.
 

Your own sample — makes User 1–4

Pick one recording of a single held note — the way the Ensoniq EPS made an instrument from one sample. Then copy the file it downloads to the root of the Pod's SD card and choose that User slot. Everything happens in this browser; nothing is uploaded.
